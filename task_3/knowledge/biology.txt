A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Cells can be prokaryotic, which lack a nucleus and are generally simpler (like bacteria), or eukaryotic, which contain a nucleus and complex organelles (like plant and animal cells). Organelles such as mitochondria, ribosomes, and the endoplasmic reticulum each have specialized roles. The cell membrane regulates the exchange of substances, while the cytoplasm provides a medium for biochemical reactions.

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and some bacteria convert sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water into glucose and oxygen. This occurs in chloroplasts, where chlorophyll absorbs light energy. The process consists of two stages: the light-dependent reactions, which generate ATP and NADPH, and the Calvin cycle, which uses these molecules to fix carbon into glucose. Photosynthesis sustains ecosystems by producing food and releasing oxygen into the atmosphere.

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that carries genetic information essential for life. Its double helix structure, discovered by Watson and Crick, is composed of nucleotide pairs: adenine with thymine, and cytosine with guanine. DNA sequences encode proteins through transcription and translation, forming the basis of cellular function. In eukaryotes, DNA is packaged into chromosomes within the nucleus, while in prokaryotes, it is found in the cytoplasm.

The human heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood throughout the body. It has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles, separated by valves that ensure one-way blood flow. The right side of the heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, while the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the rest of the body. The rhythmic contractions are controlled by electrical impulses from the sinoatrial node, often called the heart’s natural pacemaker.

Mitosis is the process of cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells. It is divided into stages: prophase (chromosomes condense), metaphase (chromosomes align), anaphase (sister chromatids separate), and telophase (nuclear membranes reform). Cytokinesis then splits the cytoplasm. Mitosis is vital for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction, ensuring that each new cell has the same genetic material as the parent.

The respiratory system enables gas exchange between the body and the environment. Air enters through the nose or mouth, passes down the trachea, and into the bronchi and lungs. Inside the lungs, alveoli provide a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse into the blood and carbon dioxide to diffuse out. Breathing is controlled by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, and regulated by the brain to maintain proper oxygen and carbon dioxide levels.

The nervous system coordinates body functions and responses to stimuli. It consists of the central nervous system (CNS), which includes the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), which connects the CNS to the rest of the body. Neurons transmit signals through electrical impulses and chemical neurotransmitters. Reflexes allow rapid responses without conscious thought, while higher brain functions enable reasoning, memory, and emotions.

The circulatory system works closely with the respiratory system to deliver oxygen and nutrients to cells and remove waste products. Blood vessels include arteries, veins, and capillaries, each specialized for transporting blood. The circulatory system also plays a role in immune defense, transporting white blood cells, and in temperature regulation by adjusting blood flow to the skin.

The digestive system breaks down food into nutrients that the body can absorb and use. It begins in the mouth with mechanical chewing and enzymatic digestion, continues in the stomach with acids and enzymes, and proceeds through the intestines where most absorption occurs. The liver and pancreas produce substances that aid digestion, while the large intestine absorbs water and forms waste. This system provides the energy and building blocks necessary for survival.

The skeletal and muscular systems provide structure, movement, and protection. Bones form the framework of the body, protect vital organs, and store minerals like calcium. Joints allow flexibility, while muscles contract to produce movement. Skeletal muscles are controlled voluntarily, while smooth and cardiac muscles function automatically. Together, these systems enable locomotion, posture, and essential bodily functions.
